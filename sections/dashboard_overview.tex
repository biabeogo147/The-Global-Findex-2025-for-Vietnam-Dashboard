\subsection{Role of Visualization in the Problem}

The raw dataset in \texttt{Findex\_Microdata\_2025\_updateViet Nam.csv}
contains 1,000 rows and 199 columns of survey responses. In raw tabular form,
the links between demographics, digital readiness, account access, payment
behavior, and resilience are difficult to compare quickly. Visualization makes
that structure readable by translating weighted responses into ordered marks
such as cells, bars, flows, and lines, so the report can show where exclusion
and cash dependence are concentrated.

\dashboardfigure{0.97}{fig-page1-overview.png}
{Overview of the Vietnam Financial Inclusion Dashboard.}
{fig:dashboard-overview}

Figure~\ref{fig:dashboard-overview} shows how the dashboard reorganizes the
problem into one policy-facing analytical flow rather than a long list of
separate indicators. This matters because the report is not only asking
whether adults have accounts, but whether readiness, access, usage, and
resilience line up or break apart across social groups.

\subsection{Types of Visualization Used}
\label{sec:dashboard-overview}

The dashboard combines explanatory and exploratory visualization. The
explanatory role appears in the fixed five-page sequence shown in
Figure~\ref{fig:dashboard-overview}: Page 1 establishes the demographic
baseline, Page 2 checks connectivity and digital identity, Page 3 examines
account ownership and exclusion barriers, Page 4 compares cash and digital
transaction flows, and Page 5 links financial access with saving, borrowing,
and coping capacity.

The exploratory role appears through Power BI filters that let readers inspect
particular subgroups without leaving that broader narrative. This hybrid
structure is appropriate for a policy audience because it keeps the national
story coherent while still supporting diagnosis at subgroup level.
